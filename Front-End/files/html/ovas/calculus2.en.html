Indefinite Integrals, Trigonometric Functions, and Fundamental Limits

A function F(x) is called an antiderivative (or primitive) of f(x) when F′(x) = f(x). The family of all antiderivatives is represented by the indefinite integral ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C, where C is the constant of integration. This duality between differentiation and integration is especially clear in trigonometric functions: since the derivative of f(x) = sin(x) is f′(x) = cos(x), it follows immediately that the indefinite integral of f(x) = cos(x) is ∫ cos(x) dx = sin(x) + C.

The very foundation of trigonometric and exponential derivatives rests on three classic 0/0 indeterminate limits, which can be evaluated through algebraic factoring, geometry, or L'Hôpital's Rule (differentiating the numerator and denominator):

Worked Example 1 (Trigonometric Derivative and Integral):
Step 1: To find the derivative of f(x) = sin(x), we use its rate of change on the unit circle: f′(x) = cos(x).
Step 2: To compute the indefinite integral of f(x) = cos(x), we look for the function whose derivative is cos(x). Since (sin(x))′ = cos(x), we conclude that ∫ cos(x) dx = sin(x) + C.

Worked Example 2 (Three Classic 0/0 Limits):
• lim(x→0) sin(x)/x: This is the fundamental trigonometric limit. As x (in radians) approaches 0, sin(x) and x exhibit the same linear behavior (or, by L'Hôpital's Rule, lim(x→0) cos(x)/1 = cos(0) = 1). Therefore, lim(x→0) sin(x)/x = 1.
• lim(x→0) (e^x − 1)/x: This is the fundamental exponential limit (which defines the derivative of e^x at x = 0). Applying L'Hôpital's Rule, we differentiate the numerator and denominator: lim(x→0) e^x / 1 = e⁰ = 1.
• lim(x→1) (x² − 1)/(x − 1): Factoring the difference of squares x² − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1) and canceling (x − 1) for x ≠ 1 gives lim(x→1) (x + 1) = 1 + 1 = 2.

Derivative of sin(x) and Integral of cos(x)

Differentiation and integration are inverse operations: the derivative of f(x) = sin(x) is cos(x), and therefore the indefinite integral of f(x) = cos(x) is sin(x) + C. Never forget to add the constant C to indefinite integrals!

Fundamental Limits as x → 0

Two remarkable limits appear throughout engineering: lim(x→0) sin(x)/x = 1 and lim(x→0) (e^x − 1)/x = 1. Both express the fact that very close to x = 0, sin(x) ≈ x and e^x − 1 ≈ x.

Difference of Squares as x → 1

To evaluate lim(x→1) (x² − 1)/(x − 1), we factor the numerator as (x − 1)(x + 1). Canceling (x − 1), we simply evaluate x + 1 at x = 1, obtaining 1 + 1 = 2.

Definite Integrals, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and Substitution

While an indefinite integral returns a family of functions, the definite integral from a to b computes the total accumulation of a quantity — geometrically, the net signed area under the curve of f(x) over the interval [a, b]. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) connects the two concepts: if F(x) is an antiderivative of f(x), then ∫ from a to b of f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a).

When the integrand is a composite expression of the form f(g(x))·g′(x), we apply u-substitution (the chain rule in reverse): define the new variable u = g(x), compute the differential du = g′(x) dx, and evaluate ∫ f(u) du.

Worked Example 1 (Area under a curve via the FTC): Find the area under the curve of f(x) = 3x² over the interval from x = 0 to x = 2.
Step 1: Find an antiderivative of f(x) = 3x²: F(x) = 3·(x³/3) = x³.
Step 2: Apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: Area = F(2) − F(0) = 2³ − 0³ = 8 − 0 = 8 square units.

Worked Example 2 (Integration by Substitution): Evaluate ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx.
Step 1: Choose u = x². Differentiating gives du = 2x dx.
Step 2: Substitute into the integral: ∫ cos(x²) · (2x dx) = ∫ cos(u) du.
Step 3: We know that ∫ cos(u) du = sin(u) + C. Substituting back in terms of x, we obtain sin(x²) + C.

The FTC turns the limit of an infinite sum of rectangles (a Riemann sum) into the simple difference F(b) − F(a) between the antiderivative's values at the endpoints of the interval.

Whenever you spot an inner function u = g(x) multiplied by its derivative g′(x) dx inside an integral, rewrite the integral in terms of u and du. Once integrated, substitute the original variable x back in.

If f(x) ≥ 0 throughout the interval [a, b], the definite integral gives the exact area between the graph of f(x) and the x-axis. In physics, the area under the velocity graph v(t) gives the total displacement.

Critical Points, Maxima, Minima, and the First and Second Derivative Tests

To analyze the behavior of quadratic and cubic polynomial curves, we first find the critical points by solving f′(x) = 0. Next, we classify each critical point using either the First Derivative Test (if f′ changes from negative to positive, we have a minimum; if it changes from positive to negative, we have a maximum) or the Second Derivative Test: we evaluate f″(x) at the critical point — if f″(x) > 0, the curve is concave up and the point is a minimum point; if f″(x) < 0, the curve is concave down and the point is a maximum point.

Worked Example 1 (Minimum of f(x) = x² − 4x + 3):
Step 1: Differentiate the function: f′(x) = 2x − 4.
Step 2: Set it equal to zero: 2x − 4 = 0 ⇒ 2x = 4 ⇒ x = 2.
Step 3: Since f″(x) = 2 > 0, the minimum point occurs at x = 2.

Worked Example 2 (Maximum of f(x) = −x² + 6x − 9):
Step 1: Compute the first derivative: f′(x) = −2x + 6.
Step 2: Set it equal to zero: −2x + 6 = 0 ⇒ 2x = 6 ⇒ x = 3.
Step 3: Since f″(x) = −2 < 0, the function has a maximum point at x = 3.

Worked Example 3 (Cubic polynomial f(x) = x³ − 3x² + 2 at x = 2):
Step 1: Compute the first derivative: f′(x) = 3x² − 6x = 3x(x − 2). Setting it equal to zero gives the critical points x = 0 and x = 2.
Step 2: Compute the second derivative: f″(x) = 6x − 6.
Step 3: Evaluate at x = 2: f″(2) = 6(2) − 6 = 12 − 6 = 6 > 0. Because f″(2) > 0, we conclude that x = 2 is a minimum point (whereas at x = 0, f″(0) = −6 < 0, which is a maximum point).

Minimum of f(x) = x² − 4x + 3

Differentiating f(x) = x² − 4x + 3 gives f′(x) = 2x − 4. Setting 2x − 4 = 0 yields x = 2. Since f″(2) = 2 > 0, the minimum point occurs at x = 2 (where f(2) = −1).

Maximum of f(x) = −x² + 6x − 9

Differentiating f(x) = −x² + 6x − 9 gives f′(x) = −2x + 6. Solving −2x + 6 = 0 leads to x = 3. Since f″(3) = −2 < 0, x = 3 is the maximum point of the function.

Cubic Curve f(x) = x³ − 3x² + 2

For f(x) = x³ − 3x² + 2, we have f′(x) = 3x² − 6x and f″(x) = 6x − 6. At the critical point x = 2, f′(2) = 0 and f″(2) = 6 > 0, confirming that x = 2 is a minimum point.

Optimization Problems in Engineering

In engineering, optimizing means designing a system to achieve maximum performance (area, volume, profit, power output) or minimum expenditure (material cost, energy loss, response time). Differential calculus solves optimization problems by turning physical constraints into a single-variable function and finding its critical points.

Worked Example (Optimizing the area of an industrial enclosure): An engineer has 120 meters of fencing to enclose a rectangular storage area against an existing wall along one side (so the fencing covers only 3 sides: two sides of width x and one side of length y). What dimensions maximize the enclosed area?
Step 1 (Constraint and objective function): The total fencing length is 2x + y = 120 ⇒ y = 120 − 2x. The area is A(x) = x · y = x(120 − 2x) = −2x² + 120x.
Step 2 (Critical point): Differentiate A(x) and set it equal to zero: A′(x) = −4x + 120 = 0 ⇒ 4x = 120 ⇒ x = 30 m.
Step 3 (Verification via the Second Derivative Test): Since A″(x) = −4 < 0, x = 30 m guarantees a maximum point. The other side measures y = 120 − 2(30) = 60 m, resulting in a maximum area of A(30) = 30 × 60 = 1,800 m².

Identify the quantity to be maximized or minimized and use the problem's constraint equation to express it as a function of a single variable f(x).

Differentiate the objective function and solve the equation f′(x) = 0 within the physically valid domain (for instance, positive lengths and times).

Confirm the nature of the extremum: f″(x) < 0 proves you have found maximum performance, whereas f″(x) > 0 proves minimum cost or waste.
