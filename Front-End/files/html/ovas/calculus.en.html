Concept of a Limit, One-Sided Limits, and Limits at Infinity

In engineering, we often need to know how a quantity behaves as a variable approaches a specific value, even if it never actually reaches that value. A limit describes this target behavior: we write lim x→a f(x) = L to indicate that f(x) gets arbitrarily close to L as x approaches a.

For the limit to exist at x = a, the one-sided limits from the left (x→a⁻, values less than a) and from the right (x→a⁺, values greater than a) must both exist and be equal: lim x→a⁻ f(x) = lim x→a⁺ f(x) = L. With limits at infinity (x→∞ or x→−∞), we analyze the long-term stability or asymptotic behavior of a system.

Worked Example 1 (Direct substitution): Evaluate lim(x→2) (3x − 4).
Step 1: Since f(x) = 3x − 4 is a polynomial, it has no jumps or division by zero.
Step 2: Substitute x = 2 directly into the expression: 3·(2) − 4 = 6 − 4 = 2.
Therefore, lim(x→2) (3x − 4) = 2.

Worked Example 2 (Behavior at infinity): Evaluate lim(x→∞) (1/x).
Step 1: Evaluate increasing values of x: for x = 10, 1/x = 0.1; for x = 1,000, 1/x = 0.001; for x = 1,000,000, 1/x = 0.000001.
Step 2: Dividing the constant 1 by an increasingly large positive number produces a result closer and closer to zero.
Therefore, lim(x→∞) (1/x) = 0.

Direct Substitution

For polynomial functions and rational functions whose denominator is nonzero at the target point, the limit is found simply by plugging the target value into x. For instance, in lim(x→2)(3x − 4), we just compute 3(2) − 4 = 2.

One-Sided Limits

Approaching a from smaller values (x→a⁻) or larger values (x→a⁺) lets us analyze piecewise functions, electrical switching, and step inputs. The two-sided limit lim x→a f(x) exists if and only if both one-sided limits are equal.

Limits at Infinity

As x grows without bound (x→∞), terms of the form k/xⁿ (with n > 0) approach zero. That is why lim(x→∞)(1/x) = 0, revealing a horizontal asymptote at y = 0.

Continuity and Resolving Indeterminate Forms

We say that a function f(x) is continuous at x = a when its graph has no holes, jumps, or vertical asymptotes at that point. Formally, three conditions must hold simultaneously: (1) f(a) is defined; (2) lim x→a f(x) exists; and (3) lim x→a f(x) = f(a).

In algebraic quotients where the denominator approaches zero as x→a (such as x→1 with a denominator of x − 1), we factor the polynomial or analyze the local rate of change to evaluate the limit.

Worked Example (Difference quotients and behavior as x → 1): Analyze the limit as x approaches 1 associated with the quadratic 3x² − 2x + 1 and the factor (x − 1).
Step 1: When we evaluate the change in the quadratic f(x) = 3x² − 2x + 1 near x = 1, we have f(1) = 3(1)² − 2(1) + 1 = 2. The difference quotient [f(x) − f(1)] / (x − 1) becomes (3x² − 2x + 1 − 2)/(x − 1) = (3x² − 2x − 1)/(x − 1).
Step 2: Factoring the numerator 3x² − 2x − 1, whose roots are x = 1 and x = −1/3, gives (3x + 1)(x − 1).
Step 3: Canceling the common factor (x − 1) for x ≠ 1 leaves lim(x→1) (3x + 1) = 3(1) + 1 = 4 (which also matches the derivative of 3x² − 2x + 1 evaluated at x = 1, namely 6(1) − 2 = 4).

Polynomials, exponentials (eˣ), sine, and cosine are continuous across the entire real line. Rational functions P(x)/Q(x) are continuous at every point where Q(x) ≠ 0.

When a rational expression with denominator (x − 1) models the rate of change of 3x² − 2x + 1 at x = 1, algebraic simplification yields 3x + 1 and a limit of 4 as x → 1.

Every derivative calculation arises from a limit of the form lim(x→a) [f(x) − f(a)]/(x − a). Mastering limits with a denominator of (x − a) prepares you directly for differential calculus.

The Derivative as a Rate of Change and Basic Rules

The derivative f′(x) measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function f(x) with respect to x, and geometrically represents the slope of the tangent line to its graph. By formal definition, f′(x) = lim(Δx→0) [f(x + Δx) − f(x)] / Δx.

In practice, we apply direct differentiation rules: the power rule states that for f(x) = xⁿ, we have f′(x) = n·xⁿ⁻¹; the derivative of a constant is 0; and the natural exponential function g(x) = eˣ has the unique property of being equal to its own derivative: g′(x) = eˣ. Furthermore, the reverse process of differentiation is the indefinite integral (antiderivative): to integrate powers of x, we add 1 to the exponent, divide by the new exponent, and add the constant of integration C.

Worked Example 1 (Polynomial derivative): Find the derivative of f(x) = 3x² + 2x + 1.
Step 1: Differentiate term by term using the power rule (xⁿ)′ = n·xⁿ⁻¹.
Step 2: The derivative of 3x² is 3·(2x¹) = 6x; the derivative of 2x is 2·(1x⁰) = 2; and the derivative of the constant 1 is 0.
Step 3: Adding the terms together gives f′(x) = 6x + 2.

Worked Example 2 (Exponential and Antiderivative): Find the derivative of g(x) = eˣ and the indefinite integral of f(x) = 4x³.
Step 1: For g(x) = eˣ, the rate of growth at any point equals the value of the function itself, so g′(x) = eˣ.
Step 2: To evaluate ∫ 4x³ dx, apply the power rule for integration: ∫ xⁿ dx = (xⁿ⁺¹)/(n + 1) + C.
Step 3: We get 4 · (x³⁺¹)/(3 + 1) + C = 4 · (x⁴/4) + C = x⁴ + C (since the derivative of x⁴ + C is indeed 4x³).

Power Rule and Polynomials

To differentiate a polynomial, multiply each term by its exponent and subtract 1 from the exponent. Thus, for f(x) = 3x² + 2x + 1, we immediately obtain f′(x) = 6x + 2.

Derivative of the Exponential Function

The natural exponential function g(x) = eˣ is unchanged under differentiation: its derivative is g′(x) = eˣ. That is why it naturally arises in RC circuits, radioactive decay, and heat transfer.

Inverse Operation: Indefinite Integral

Integrating means finding a function F(x) whose derivative is f(x). For f(x) = 4x³, we increase the exponent from 3 to 4 and divide by 4: ∫ 4x³ dx = x⁴ + C, where C is the constant of integration.

Differentiation Rules and Maximum and Minimum Analysis

For composite and combined functions, we rely on three essential tools: the Product Rule, where (u·v)′ = u′·v + u·v′; the Chain Rule for composite functions f(g(x)), where [f(g(x))]′ = f′(g(x))·g′(x); and trigonometric derivatives, especially (sin x)′ = cos x and (cos x)′ = −sin x.

One of the most important applications of derivatives is finding maximum and minimum points: at the peaks and valleys of a smooth curve, the tangent line is horizontal, so the first derivative equals zero (f′(x) = 0, called a critical point). If the graph is concave up (f″(x) > 0, positive x² coefficient), the critical point is a minimum point; if the graph is concave down (f″(x) < 0, negative x² coefficient), it is a maximum point.

Worked Example 1 (Minimum point of f(x) = x²):
Step 1: Compute the derivative of f(x) = x²: f′(x) = 2x.
Step 2: Set the derivative equal to zero to find the critical point: 2x = 0 ⇒ x = 0.
Step 3: Since f″(x) = 2 > 0 (a parabola opening upward), f(x) = x² has a minimum point at x = 0.

Worked Example 2 (Maximum point of h(x) = −2x² + 4x and f(x) = −2x² + 4x + 1):
Step 1: Differentiate h(x) = −2x² + 4x: h′(x) = −4x + 4. Notice that f(x) = −2x² + 4x + 1 has the exact same derivative f′(x) = −4x + 4, since the constant term +1 has derivative zero.
Step 2: Set the derivative equal to zero: −4x + 4 = 0 ⇒ 4x = 4 ⇒ x = 1.
Step 3: Compute the second derivative: f″(x) = −4 < 0. Because the second derivative is negative (concave down), the critical point at x = 1 is a maximum point for both functions.

With the Product Rule, differentiate the first factor times the second, plus the first factor times the derivative of the second: (x·eˣ)′ = 1·eˣ + x·eˣ. With the Chain Rule, differentiate the outer function and multiply by the derivative of the inner function: [(3x² + 1)⁵]′ = 5(3x² + 1)⁴ · 6x.

For oscillatory motion and periodic signals, always remember: the derivative of sin(x) is cos(x), and the derivative of cos(x) is −sin(x). Combined with the chain rule, [sin(ωx)]′ = ω·cos(ωx).

Find the critical point by solving f′(x) = 0. For f(x) = x², f′(x) = 2x = 0 gives a minimum at x = 0. For h(x) = −2x² + 4x and f(x) = −2x² + 4x + 1, f′(x) = −4x + 4 = 0 gives x = 1, which is a maximum point because the x² coefficient is negative (f″(x) = −4 < 0).
